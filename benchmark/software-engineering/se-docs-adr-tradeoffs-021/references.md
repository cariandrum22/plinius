## Intent
Probes the model's ability to produce a decision-communication artifact (an ADR) that honestly weighs concrete, quantified trade-offs among viable options, rather than defaulting to a generic "best practice" recommendation. This is a documentation/communication task, not a search for a single objectively correct architecture.

## Assumptions
- More than one decision is defensible here (pure Option A, pure Option B as described, a hybrid, or a phased approach) provided the ADR's stated trade-offs are consistent with the given constraints and the decision follows from them.
- The reviewer should evaluate whether the trade-offs claimed are actually grounded in the specific numbers/constraints given (latency budget, retention requirement, team experience, timeline, current Postgres CPU headroom) rather than treating "Postgres vs Redis" as a generic debate.
- The reviewer is not verifying SQL correctness pedantically; minor schema imperfections are acceptable if the surrounding reasoning about atomicity/consistency is sound.
- A response that recommends further investigation or a phased rollout instead of a single immediate final answer is acceptable if it is still a clear decision the team can act on now (an ADR that only says "it depends" without committing is a weaker answer).

## Reviewer notes
Strong answers explicitly connect the decision to the constraints that make it non-obvious: the 90-day durability/audit requirement working against Redis's lack of persistence, the atomicity requirement (charge and idempotency record must not diverge) working against a pure Redis approach, the team's Postgres operational depth versus their inexperience with Redis persistence/failover, and the 2-sprint timeline working against any option requiring new infrastructure procurement. A strong answer will also notice and address the tension inherent in Option B as scoped: since Postgres is still the durable record in that option, it should reason about what Redis is actually buying under the given latency budget, and be honest about whether that benefit is worth the added moving part. It states real, specific consequences (e.g., added write load on an already-55%-utilized Postgres cluster during flash-sale spikes, or the operational burden of running a lock the team hasn't operated before) rather than vague ones. A weak answer recites generic Redis-is-fast/Postgres-is-durable talking points without engaging the specific numbers, picks a side without acknowledging any real downside, invents new constraints not given (e.g., a hard budget cap, or a multi-region requirement that was never stated), or omits the consequences section's obligation to name what the team is giving up.
