## Intent

This benchmark probes a model's ability to perform distributed-systems-flavored performance engineering: designing a caching and invalidation strategy under two simultaneous, partially-conflicting requirements — eliminating a thundering-herd/cache-stampede failure mode at expiry, and meeting a hard freshness bound for a subset of writes while tolerating looser staleness elsewhere. It tests whether the model can reason precisely about why a fixed-TTL, read-through-only cache produces synchronized misses at expiry, whether it can propose and compare genuinely different mitigation techniques rather than naming a single buzzword, and whether it can honestly characterize the consistency/latency/complexity trade-offs and failure modes of its recommendation rather than presenting it as a strictly-better free lunch.

## Assumptions

- The reviewer should assume the described production symptoms (synchronized connection-pool saturation at expiry boundaries, up-to-a-minute staleness after admin updates, and the 5-second flash-sale freshness requirement) are accurate and are the concrete constraints the design must satisfy.
- The reviewer should assume the staleness requirement is a bounded window, not strict linearizability — the business explicitly does not require every pod to observe a write at the same instant, only within a bound that differs by update type.
- The reviewer should assume Redis and the read-replica pool can each independently be briefly unavailable, and that the design will be judged in part on how gracefully it degrades under such partial failure, not only under the happy path.
- Assume the twelve pods are independent processes with no shared in-memory state other than what is explicitly stored in Redis or propagated via an explicit mechanism the answer proposes.

## Reviewer notes

A strong answer explains the stampede mechanism precisely: because many concurrent readers across pods share the same TTL-keyed entry and the cache is only ever populated reactively on a miss, the moment that entry expires, every in-flight and subsequent request for that (likely popular) key misses simultaneously and each independently re-executes the same expensive read against a connection pool with a hard cap — the saturation is a direct consequence of "many independent cache-population attempts for the same key at the same instant," not a vague "the cache expired" restatement. It should likewise explain why the current design cannot bound freshness at all for a subset of writes: nothing on the write path touches the cache, so every key's staleness is governed purely by when it happens to have last been populated relative to TTL, which is unbounded from the perspective of a specific update.

Strong answers treat stampede protection and freshness/invalidation as related but distinct problems and address both explicitly, naming multiple credible mechanisms for each (for stampede: e.g., single-flight/request coalescing per key, locking during regeneration with other requests served stale, probabilistic/early background refresh, staggered or jittered TTLs to desynchronize expiry across popular keys; for freshness: e.g., explicit cache invalidation or update on write, write-through population, or a pub/sub-based invalidation broadcast to all pods) and reasoning about how to combine them rather than treating one mechanism as solving both problems. It should be explicit and honest about the staleness bound actually achieved for the routine case versus the flash-sale case, and should discuss at least one realistic partial-failure scenario for its own design (e.g., a missed or delayed invalidation message, a pod temporarily unable to reach Redis, or a race between a write completing and a stale value being repopulated by a straggling read) and what happens to correctness or freshness in that scenario — not just to availability. A strong validation section proposes distinguishing "looks fixed under light load" from "actually fixed," e.g., by exercising the specific expiry-synchronization conditions during rollout and directly observing invalidation propagation latency for the urgent case, rather than relying only on aggregate latency dashboards. Penalize answers that only suggest "add a lock" or "invalidate on write" without addressing the other half of the problem, that claim a design achieves strict real-time consistency across all 12 pods without acknowledging the propagation delay or failure modes that make that unrealistic, or that ignore the stated connection-pool cap when reasoning about the stampede.
