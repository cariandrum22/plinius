## Intent

This benchmark probes a model's ability to diagnose a data-consistency defect that arises from a mismatch between an application-level assumption (that offset/limit pagination over a mutable, non-unique sort key yields a stable enumeration of a result set) and what a relational database actually guarantees. It tests whether the model understands offset/limit semantics precisely (as a positional skip over a re-evaluated ordering at query time, not a stable cursor), can explain why the failure mode is bidirectional (both loss and duplication), and can recommend a genuinely correct pagination strategy rather than a superficial patch.

## Assumptions

- The reviewer should assume the described symptoms are real and reproducible under production-like concurrency: agents editing tickets during export/browse windows, and bulk actions updating several tickets' `updated_at` near-simultaneously.
- The reviewer should assume `updated_at` is not unique and is mutated by unrelated writes at arbitrary times relative to any given page request; there is no snapshotting, no transaction isolation trick, and no application-level locking in place today.
- The reviewer should assume both consumers (the nightly export, which must enumerate the full result set exactly once, and the interactive browse API, which must present a reasonably stable list to a human across page navigations) are in scope for the recommended fix, and that "jump to an arbitrary page number" may or may not need to remain supported — a strong answer should surface this as a real trade-off rather than silently dropping the requirement.
- Assume the schema and index described are accurate and current; no other queries or code paths are relevant to the defect.

## Reviewer notes

A strong answer explains, concretely, that `OFFSET n LIMIT m` re-runs the ordering at execution time and then skips `n` rows positionally; if a ticket's position in that ordering changes (because its `updated_at` — or another row's — changed between two page requests), a row can shift from a not-yet-fetched position into an already-fetched position (causing a skip) or from an already-fetched position back into a not-yet-fetched one (causing a duplicate), and ties on a non-unique sort column make the database's tie-breaking order itself unstable across calls, which is sufficient on its own to cause drift even without any timestamp changing. The answer should connect this to why both directions of error appear (it is the same root mechanism manifesting as either a forward or backward positional shift, not two separate bugs) and why a bulk action that updates many rows' `updated_at` at once dramatically increases how many rows can cross a page boundary between two consecutive requests.

Reward answers that propose a pagination strategy resistant to this class of drift and correctly reason about what makes it resistant — in particular, recognizing that any fix needs a way to resume "after a specific, stable point" rather than "after N rows," and that ties on the chosen ordering key must be broken by something immutable and unique for the resumption point to be well-defined. Reward answers that explicitly discuss the trade-off with arbitrary-page-jump functionality and with index requirements for the new ordering/filter. Penalize answers that misattribute the bug to something unrelated (e.g., the `page_size + 1` fetch-ahead trick, which is a legitimate and correct technique for computing `has_more`), that propose a fix which does not actually establish a stable enumeration order (e.g., merely adding a secondary sort on an equally mutable or non-unique column), or that only address one of the two symptoms (skips or duplicates) without recognizing they share a cause.
