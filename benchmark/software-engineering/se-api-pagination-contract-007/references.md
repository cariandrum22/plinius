## Intent

This benchmark probes a model's ability to design a public, evolvable pagination and filtering contract for a real production collection endpoint. It specifically tests whether the model can (1) reason precisely about what "correct pagination" means under concurrent writes rather than treating pagination as a purely cosmetic API shape question, (2) identify and resolve the tie-breaking and ordering-stability issues that cause skipped or duplicated results in naive offset-based designs, (3) make and justify a defensible trade-off between competing pagination mechanisms rather than asserting one approach is unconditionally superior, and (4) treat backward compatibility with existing external integrators as a first-class constraint on the design rather than an afterthought.

## Assumptions

- The reviewer should assume the transactions table is large and actively written to (inserts of new transactions, in-place status updates) concurrently with clients paging through it.
- The reviewer should assume external, third-party integrators currently depend on the documented `offset`/`limit`/`total` response shape, and that the organization's stated policy prohibits breaking them without a deprecation window — so a design that silently changes the existing contract in place, with no migration path, does not satisfy the deliverable.
- The reviewer should assume "sort by amount" (or any non-unique column) can produce ties among multiple rows, and that the underlying store only guarantees ordering the client explicitly requests via indexed columns.
- The reviewer should assume the model may choose any pagination mechanism (cursor-based, keyset, hybrid, or a well-justified refinement of offset-based pagination), provided the choice is explicit and its consequences are reasoned through, not merely asserted.

## Reviewer notes

A strong answer states an explicit, falsifiable consistency guarantee for what a client walking every page will observe (for example, with respect to items inserted, deleted, or mutated while paging), rather than leaving "correctness under concurrent writes" implicit or aspirational. It resolves the tie-breaking problem for non-unique sort columns concretely, so that page boundaries are well-defined even when many rows share a sort key. It treats the response envelope, pagination token/parameter design, and error taxonomy as parts of one coherent contract rather than addressing them in isolation, and it engages honestly with what is sacrificed by its chosen mechanism (such as the ability to jump to an arbitrary page, or the cost/availability of an exact total count at scale) instead of presenting its choice as strictly dominant with no downsides. It also proposes a credible way for existing integrators to keep working during a transition, rather than describing only the end-state contract as if it could be deployed atomically without disruption.

A weaker answer keeps the existing offset/limit/total shape but only patches symptoms (e.g., "add a secondary sort key" without addressing the compatibility or total-count-at-scale problems), proposes a new contract with no migration story for existing clients, hand-waves the concurrency guarantee ("cursors fix this" without saying what is guaranteed), or omits a meaningful discussion of error/edge cases such as malformed pagination state, unsupported sort columns, or limits exceeding a maximum.
