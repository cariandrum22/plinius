## Intent

This benchmark probes a model's ability to reason about the operational correctness of a concurrent worker pool beyond "does it compile and run": specifically, whether unbounded producer/consumer mismatch is recognized as a memory-safety-adjacent liveness problem (backpressure), and whether shutdown is recognized as a distinct correctness property with its own failure modes (lost work, indefinite blocking) that must be designed for explicitly rather than assumed to fall out of "stop the loop and join the threads." It tests diagnosis of two related but separable defects in the same piece of code and the ability to produce a redesign with an explicit, defensible draining policy rather than a vague "make it graceful" gesture.

## Assumptions

- The reviewer should assume standard Rust `std::sync::mpsc` semantics: an `mpsc::channel()` (as used here) is unbounded, `Sender::send` on it does not block the caller, and a value sitting in the channel is dropped (not silently magically delivered) when the channel and its remaining handles are dropped without being received.
- The reviewer should assume the described production symptoms (unbounded memory growth under spike load recovering after restart; silently lost jobs submitted near a deploy; rare deploy hangs past the grace period) are accurate telemetry to be explained, not red herrings, and that both problems stem from this code as shown, not from something outside it.
- The reviewer should assume `process` genuinely performs blocking I/O of variable, sometimes multi-second duration, and that this is a real constraint the redesign must account for rather than assume away.
- The reviewer should assume `num_workers` is fixed and materially smaller than the peak submission rate during spikes, i.e., the pool is genuinely under sustained overload at times, not just momentarily bursty.

## Reviewer notes

A strong answer will tie the unbounded memory growth precisely to the choice of an unbounded `mpsc::channel` combined with a `submit` that never exerts any pressure back on the caller: every submitted `Job` (including its `payload: Vec<u8>`) is retained in the channel's internal queue for as long as production outpaces the fixed number of workers' consumption rate, so during a spike the backlog — and the memory it holds — grows linearly with the excess of arrivals over service capacity, bounded only by process memory. It should be explicit that CPU staying saturated-but-stable is consistent with workers being fully busy while the queue still grows, i.e., raw worker throughput being fixed is exactly the condition under which an unbounded queue is dangerous.

For the shutdown defects, a strong answer will notice that the stop condition is checked only at the top of each worker's loop, before it attempts to pull the next job, so any job already sitting in the channel but not yet claimed at the moment `stop` flips to true is never retrieved by that worker and is simply discarded once the channel is dropped — this is the mechanism for silently lost jobs near a deploy, and it should be described as a draining failure, not a race in the traditional data-race sense. Separately, a strong answer will notice that `shutdown` joins each worker thread unconditionally, and a worker that is presently inside a call to `process` (blocking I/O against object storage) will not observe the stop flag until that call returns, so if the backend is slow or briefly unavailable at the moment of shutdown, `join` can block for as long as that call takes — this is the plausible mechanism for an occasional hang past the termination grace period, and a strong answer should connect it to the fact that nothing in the current design distinguishes "let in-flight work finish" from "wait unconditionally, however long that takes."

Reward redesigns that make an explicit, stated choice for each of the following rather than leaving them implicit: (a) a bounded queue with a stated capacity and a stated policy for what happens when it is full — blocking the submitter (and what that does to caller-side latency/timeouts), rejecting/failing fast with a signal the caller can act on, or a bounded drop policy — since "bounded" alone is not a complete answer without saying what happens at the bound; (b) a shutdown protocol that stops accepting new submissions immediately, distinguishes already-queued-but-unclaimed jobs from job in progress, and states what happens to each (e.g., finish all queued work versus abandon queued work but let in-flight jobs complete versus a hard deadline after which remaining work is abandoned); and (c) whether/how a maximum shutdown wait is enforced given that `process` can legitimately take seconds, so that the earlier hang scenario cannot simply reappear under the new design. Penalize answers that add a bounded channel but do not address what happens when it is full, that claim graceful shutdown is achieved without describing draining semantics for both queued and in-flight jobs, or that ignore the fact that in-flight blocking I/O cannot be interrupted from the outside in this model and so must be bounded by a deadline rather than assumed to always finish quickly.
